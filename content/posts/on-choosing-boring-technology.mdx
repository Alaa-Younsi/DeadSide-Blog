Every new project comes with a small, seductive question: what if this is the one where we try the thing? The new framework, the new database, the language that promises to make the next six months easier than the last six were. Sometimes the promise is real. Most of the time, what actually gets purchased with that novelty is risk — paid for later, usually by whoever has to debug it at two in the morning with no community threads to search.

"Boring" doesn't mean bad. It means well-understood: the failure modes are documented, the workarounds are a search away, and the third engineer to touch the codebase can recognize the shape of it immediately. Boring technology has already had its rough edges sanded down by everyone who adopted it before you did. That's not a lack of ambition. It's borrowed stability.

The trick is spending that stability budget deliberately. A project gets a small number of genuinely novel bets before the accumulated unfamiliarity starts working against it. Spend them on the part of the problem that's actually new — the part unique to what you're building — and let everything around it be as unremarkable as possible. The goal was never to avoid interesting problems. It was to make sure the interesting problems are the ones you chose on purpose.
