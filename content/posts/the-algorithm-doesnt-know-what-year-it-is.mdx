Open any feed and you'll find three timelines colliding at once: something from an hour ago, something from last year, and something with no discernible date at all, all presented with equal confidence. The recommendation engine doesn't experience time the way we do. It experiences engagement, and engagement doesn't decay on a calendar — it decays on a curve, and that curve doesn't care whether the underlying event happened yesterday or three years ago.

This produces a strange kind of cultural static. A joke from an old show resurfaces stripped of its context and gets read as current commentary. A tragedy gets recommended next to something trivial, not because a person chose that pairing but because two unrelated engagement curves happened to intersect. We've built machines that are exceptional at knowing what will hold our attention for the next ninety seconds and functionally illiterate about what year it is.

<PullQuote>
  The feed doesn't lie to you. It just doesn't know the difference between a memory and a headline.
</PullQuote>

None of this is a call to abandon recommendation systems — they're remarkably good at their actual job. It's a reminder that "relevant" and "recent" are different axes, and most of the interfaces we use every day have quietly collapsed them into one. The next genuinely useful step in media design might not be a smarter algorithm. It might just be a timestamp treated as a first-class citizen again, not an afterthought buried under three taps.
