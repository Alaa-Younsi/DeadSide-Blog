This edition of the paper runs on nothing but static files. There's no database behind it, no admin panel, no login screen — writing a new story means adding a Markdown file to a folder and pushing it to a repository. That sounds almost too plain for something meant to look and move like a real newspaper, but the two goals turned out to line up cleanly: a paper's content doesn't change after it's printed, and a statically generated site doesn't change after it's built.

Every article is an `.mdx` file with a small amount of frontmatter at the top:

```yaml
title: "Building a Newspaper With No Backend"
category: experiments
date: "2026-09-19"
featured: true
```

That frontmatter is validated at build time against a schema, not guessed at runtime — a typo in a category name fails the build instead of quietly rendering a broken page in production. The category desks, the front-page lead story, and the "also in this edition" briefs are all derived from that same typed data, which means adding a new section of the paper is a one-line change to a config file, not a new set of hand-wired pages.

<PullQuote>
  The constraint wasn't "no backend" for its own sake. It was: nothing should be able to go down except the CDN.
</PullQuote>

The tradeoff is honest: there's no way to publish from a phone, no live editing, no comments stored anywhere. In exchange, the entire site is just HTML, CSS, and a handful of prebuilt routes — fast by default, with nothing to patch, nothing to back up, and nothing that can be down except the network in front of it. For a paper that exists to be read, that trade was worth making.
