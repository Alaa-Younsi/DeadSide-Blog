For most of the last decade, "the cloud" was simply where software lived. You didn't buy a server, you rented compute by the second, and the whole conversation about infrastructure quietly disappeared into someone else's data center. That trade made sense when growth was the only metric that mattered and engineering time was more expensive than almost anything else.

That calculus is shifting. A small but noisy wave of teams — indie developers, tiny studios, a handful of mid-sized companies — are pulling workloads back onto machines they can touch. Not everything. Nobody is racking servers to run a marketing site. But the steady-state workloads, the ones that don't spike, the ones that have run the same way for two years and will run the same way for two more — those are coming home.

<PullQuote cite="a systems engineer, on why the migration happened">
  We weren't chasing a trend. We just added up the invoice for eighteen months and bought the hardware outright with the difference.
</PullQuote>

The honest reason is cost, and cost alone. Egress fees, managed-service markups, and the compounding tax of "just one more add-on" turn a linear workload into an exponential bill. A machine that costs four thousand dollars once, and draws forty watts a day, starts to look very different from a line item that renews every single month, forever, with no ceiling.

None of this is an argument against the cloud. Elastic scaling, managed databases, and global edge networks solve real problems that a rack in a closet cannot. But it's a reminder that "the cloud" was never a philosophy — it was a pricing model, and pricing models change behavior. The interesting infrastructure decisions of the next few years won't be cloud versus not-cloud. They'll be about which workloads actually need what the cloud is selling, and which ones are just paying rent out of habit.
